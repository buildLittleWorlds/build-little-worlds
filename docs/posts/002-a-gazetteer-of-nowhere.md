*Selected entries from the first survey of a country that exists only while it is being computed.*

**How to read this gazetteer.** The country has thirty-six settlements, all of them words, organized into six provinces: Animals, Places, Foods, Tools, Feelings, Ideas. It was surveyed on July 5, 2026, by a small embedding model — all-MiniLM-L6-v2, about thirty megabytes, the kind that runs inside a browser tab — which was shown each word and asked, in effect, where it keeps it. The model answers in 384 numbers. No page can draw 384 dimensions, so the survey flattens them to two, choosing the single viewing angle that pulls the six provinces farthest apart. Every flat map of a larger world lies somewhere; this one, pressing 384 dimensions into 2, lies nearly everywhere, and says so. Coordinates are given in the country's own units and mean nothing outside this atlas. Distances are measured on the flat map, not in the deep space; the entries will tell you when that matters.

One more caution before the border. The country has no north. The two axes are directions the mathematics found useful, nothing more. The words at the top of the map are not higher, only elsewhere.

## Selected entries

### bread · Foods

```
(-0.153, -0.176)   nearest settlement: engine (Tools), 0.017 away
```

The westernmost food in the country, and its nearest neighbor is not a food. Across a border no one can see, seventeen thousandths of a map-unit away, sits engine — and each is the other's nearest neighbor. The surveyor suspects the bread machine — some appliance of prose in which the two words spent years side by side — but the record only says: here is bread, and beside it, humming, an engine.

### cat · Animals — provincial capital

## Surveyor's note: the seven disputed households

Twenty-nine of the thirty-six words have a nearest neighbor from their own province; the flat map keeps the provinces mostly whole. Seven live nearest a foreigner: bread beside engine, engine beside bread, apple beside ladder, ladder beside apple, dog beside apple, and hammer and needle both drawn toward pepper. Foods and Tools account for nearly all of it, which seems right — the kitchen is the one room where those two provinces were never separate.

Honesty requires a second sentence. These are flat-map facts. The projection was chosen to pull the provinces apart, and it paid for that clarity in border towns; in the deep space the disputes may sit elsewhere, or nowhere. A map that optimizes for anything will be wrong about something, and this one is wrong, in a documented direction, at its edges.

## Surveyor's note: where the country is

Nowhere. The oldest name for an ideal country already admitted it was no-place, and this one makes the admission a matter of engineering. The country has no territory. When the map is opened, a browser fetches the model, the model re-reads all thirty-six words, and the survey runs again from nothing — same words, same method, the whole country founded fresh on whatever machine happened to ask. Close the tab and it is gone. Open two tabs and there are, briefly, two of it, identical and unaware of each other.

This is why the gazetteer states its date and its model the way older atlases stated an edition. The survey pins its instrument to one version, because when the model's next version arrives, every coordinate in this gazetteer moves at once — the whole country shifts under its settlements overnight, and no notice is posted. What you are reading is not the map of a stable land. It is one dated printing of a land that exists only in printings.

And if some evening the model cannot be fetched at all — the network absent, the shelf empty — what remains is exactly this: the printed survey, dated, exact, and already a little old. The fallback of the live country is the gazetteer you are holding.

## Surveyor's note: on the uses of a country that does not exist

Two, so far.

The first is the standing gift that any word — your name, a color, a small regret — already has a place here. The map was complete before any question was put to it; surveying only visits. Type a word into the live instrument and you do not add a settlement, you discover one that was always zoned.

The second is stranger. The six provinces were the surveyor's idea; the model was told which words were Animals and which were Ideas the way any census imposes its categories. But the neighborhoods were nobody's idea. Nobody decided that bread should live beside engine, that chaos should live beside freedom, that honey should be caught mid-strait between the table and the person across it. Those adjacencies were found, not planned — the sediment of everything the model read. The provinces are administration. The neighborhoods are the country.

Errata will be treated as border adjustments.
